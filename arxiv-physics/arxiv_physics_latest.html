What is the intrinsic reason of emergence of macroscopic order parameter out of microscopic systems? In this article, we apply to physics the collective predictive coding (CPC), the theory of language/symbol emergence. As our language emerges out of our human communications, physical macroscopic order parameter emerges out of communications among atoms --- each atom is regarded as an agent who predicts each other's behavior through inference. We present three examples of classical spin systems: two-dimensional ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic Ising models, a two-dimensional spin model with competing stripe orders, and the fully connected Sherrington--Kirkpatrick spin-glass model. In all of these examples, proper macroscopic order parameters including the replica overlap emerge automatically through communication informatics of CPC, only from observed spin configurations. We call the learned macroscopic coordinate the collective predictive order. Furthermore, for the ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic Ising models, the equilibrium distribution of the collective predictive order yields an emergent potential with the symmetry and thermal evolution characteristic of a Landau potential. Notably, the emergence of the collective predictive order does not refer to any structure of microscopic Hamiltonians/Lagrangians. Thus our ``Atom-as-an-Agent" (AaaA) principle offers a completely data-driven method for the emergence of macroscopic order, that is, machine learning physics.

Density estimation with generative models is a common task in the physical sciences, for example in background estimation in particle physics. Existing machine learning techniques, however, lack methods to quantify uncertainties that can be propagated into a statistical test. That limits the validation of generative models to standard goodness-of-fit tests, in which the generative model is treated as exact (point-null hypothesis). But a model fitted to a finite training set is never exact, so if its uncertainties are neglected, any sufficiently powerful goodness-of-fit test will eventually reject it once the test set is large enough to resolve this estimation error. We propose SIGMA, an end-to-end framework combining density estimation with principled uncertainty quantification. We use Gaussian mixture models, for which uncertainties are tractable, and further construct a new goodness-of-fit test in which these uncertainties are profiled as nuisance parameters to account for the statistical error of the fit, promoting the conventional point-null test to a composite one. A rejection then signals genuine mismodeling rather than a test set large enough to resolve the error of the fit, provided the propagated uncertainties faithfully cover that error. We demonstrate the framework on a controlled two-dimensional toy model and on a collider physics benchmark modeling the QCD background process. With uncertainties included, the same model can be trusted on test sets up to an order of magnitude larger than the standard test allows. Our work thus provides a statistically grounded basis for a robust use of generative models in the physical sciences.

Lund-plane and Particle Transformer (ParT) jet taggers can differ in performance because one representation is weaker, or because a weaker downstream computation reads an adequate one. Standalone performance cannot separate these. Model stitching can: it links the frozen early layers of one network to the frozen late layers of another through a single trained linear map. As a positive control we use an incomplete Lund representation, the primary declustering branch, whose deficit is known independently. Stitched into ParT's downstream, it reaches a background rejection at least as much as its own downstream recovers, yet far below ParT's background rejection: the deficit is representational, not computational. A hypergraph reader over splitting triplets does no better, ruling out higher-order correlations as the cause. The gap is invisible in AUC, which stays within 1% of its target value, and appears only in background rejection at low signal efficiency. It localizes to 0.6% of background jets, at and above the top mass, with more constituents but fewer primary splittings than typical background. Rebuilding the representation on the full Cambridge/Aachen declustering tree repairs the deficit as predicted: standalone rejection rises, closing 72% of the gap. Masking nodes at inference shows the repaired network still relies almost entirely on the primary branch; the secondary splittings make the representation learnable rather than supplying information it reads. Along the primary branch, the widest-angle splitting, near the top and $W$ opening angles, is worth nearly twice a random one. For quark/gluon tagging the primary branch is far less deficient, retaining 77.6% of ParT's rejection (46.9% for top), and the full tree closes only 33% of the gap (72% for top).

We analyze the on-shell content of the local Lorentz covariants of the five-dimensional spin-zero Duffin-Kemmer-Petiau algebra in elastic spin-zero-spin-$1/2$ scattering. The scalar, vector, and tensor structures are reduced explicitly to the two independent amplitudes allowed by the physical parity-conserving process. The two scalar structures are proportional on shell, the genuine nonminimal vector $i[P,β^μ]$ is longitudinal and vanishes after contraction with the conserved fermion vector current, and the projected vectors $Pβ^μ$ and $β^μP$ each reduce to one half of the minimal-vector contribution. The tensor covariants introduce no additional on-shell directions. We discuss the consequences for the four invariant choices used in meson-nucleus optical-potential calculations and show that different nuclear equations can arise only after on-shell-equivalent representatives are promoted to optical operators and iterated beyond the order at which the elementary impulse approximation is fixed. These results provide a systematic basis for distinguishing genuinely independent elementary amplitudes from representation-dependent effects generated by the optical embedding.

Magnetic null points define the magnetic field topology in stellar coronae, planetary magnetospheres, and astrospheres, and are commonly associated with magnetic reconnection. We investigate the local structure of stationary three-dimensional (3D) resistive, incompressible magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) flows in the vicinity of an isolated magnetic null and an isolated velocity stagnation point, assuming that the two nulls are colocated. The governing MHD equations are solved self-consistently using a first-order local expansion of the magnetic and velocity fields about the null. For any given magnetic-null configuration, the equations admit a two-parameter family of stationary solutions, comprising multiple solution branches with distinct velocity topologies. Along these families, the velocity-null polarity can change at bifurcation points where the flow becomes effectively 2.5D. A single magnetic null can therefore support positive-radial, negative-radial, and spiral stagnation flows, with qualitatively different inflow, outflow, and rotational properties. The associated pressure and resistivity distributions are likewise determined self-consistently and vary across the solution family, demonstrating that they are coupled to the flow topology rather than prescribed independently. The generic solutions do not require the velocity and magnetic separatrices to coincide, allowing plasma to cross the magnetic fan separatrix and providing a local reconnective flow.
